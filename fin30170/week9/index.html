From a founder's savings to a stock market listing, each type of investor brings money and asks for something in return.

The big picture

Where a company gets its money

A new company starts with the founders' own money. As it grows, it needs more cash than the founders have, so it brings in outside investors.

Each step up the ladder brings in more money. In return, the new investors ask for a bigger say and better protection.

Words to know

Equity
Ownership of a company, in the form of shares.
Valuation
What investors agree the company is worth.
Dilution
When new shares are issued, each existing owner holds a smaller percentage.
Exit
How investors get their money back: selling the company, or listing it on a stock market.
IPO
Initial public offering: the first sale of a company's shares to the public.

Venture capital

Betting on young companies

Venture capital (VC) is money invested in young companies that could grow very fast, in exchange for a share of the company. Banks rarely lend to these firms: they have few assets to offer as security, little or no profit, and a good chance of failing.

A VC investor accepts that risk because the reward from a single success can be huge. In 1999, the VC firms Sequoia Capital and Kleiner Perkins invested $25 million in a small search company called Google. Five years later, Google listed on the stock market.

VCs do more than write cheques. They usually join the board, help hire managers, introduce customers and prepare the company for its next round of funding.

The money does not belong to the VC firm itself. It comes from a fund, which raises money from large investors and must give it back, with a profit, after about ten years.

Ask Gemini

How does the '2 and 20' fee in a venture capital fund work? Use a €100m fund as a simple example, and explain why it pushes VCs towards a few very big wins.

A VC fund does not need every company to succeed. It needs one big winner.

Only 2 or 3 in 10 early investments succeed (BMAE 14-1). Several lose everything. The fund still does well because one company returns many times its cost and pays for all the losses.

This is why VCs look for companies that could become very large, not ones that will grow steadily. A safe, modest business cannot produce the big winner the fund needs.

How much of the company does the investor get?

The value of the company before the new money arrives is the pre-money value. Add the new money and you get the post-money value.

The investor's share is the new money divided by the post-money value. Try changing the numbers.

The option-pool catch

Start-ups set aside new shares for future staff, called an option pool. Investors often ask for it to be created before they invest.

Example: €2m at €8m pre-money, plus a 10% pool. The investor still gets 20%, but the founders keep 70%, not 80%. Their shares are worth €7m, not €8m.

So the pool is paid for by the existing owners, not the new investor.

Money arrives in stages

VCs do not hand over all the money at once. A company raises a round, uses it to reach a goal, then raises the next round at a higher value.

Staging protects the investor: if the company misses its goal, the VC can stop funding it.

Each new round also dilutes the earlier owners, as the chart shows. Founders accept this because a smaller share of a valuable company is worth more than all of a company that runs out of cash.

Seen from the founders' side

A goal that is too hard, or due too soon, can stop the next payment and leave the company short of cash. And if later payments are made at today's price, the founders sell shares cheaply even after the company has become more valuable.

Deal terms

The price is only part of the deal

Before investing, a VC sends a term sheet: a short document listing the main conditions of the deal. It is tempting to look only at the valuation, but these five terms decide who gets what when things go well, and when they go badly.

1Who gets paid firstIf the company is sold, the VC gets its money back before the founders get anything. Called a liquidation preference.
2Protection against a lower priceIf the company later sells shares more cheaply, the VC receives extra shares. Called anti-dilution.
3Board seats and vetoesThe VC sits on the board and must agree to big decisions, such as selling the company or raising more money.
4VestingFounders earn their shares over several years, so a founder who leaves early can lose some of them.
5Forced saleIf most shareholders agree to sell the company, the others must sell too. Called a drag-along.

Why "who gets paid first" matters

A VC pays €4m for 40% of a company. The founders own the other 60%. A few years later the company is sold for only €6m.

With simple shares, the €6m is split 40:60. If the VC is paid first, it takes its €4m back before the founders see anything. If the VC is paid first and then shares in the rest, it gets even more.

Same company, same sale price. The only difference is one line in the term sheet.

Three dials on "who gets paid first"

A liquidation preference is not one fixed rule. Three settings decide how much the investor takes, and each one can be negotiated.

AThe multipleAt 1×, the investor gets its money back once before the founders. At 2×, it gets twice its money first.
BParticipationAfter being paid first, does the investor stop there, or also share in what is left? Sharing as well is sometimes called double dipping.
CConversionAn investor that does not participate can instead swap its preferred shares for ordinary shares. It does this when the company sells for a lot, because its percentage is then worth more.

Some deals also add a yearly dividend, for example 8% a year, that builds up and is paid on top when the company is sold.

The table shows the same €4m investment for 40% under three settings. The dials matter most when the sale price is low. When the price is high, the investor's percentage matters more.

Control

Owning shares is not the same as running the company

Control comes from three places: votes at shareholder meetings, seats on the board, and vetoes written into the deal.

The board hires and fires the chief executive (CEO) and approves big decisions. So a founder with 40% of the shares can still run the company if the founders hold most board seats and the CEO job. A founder with 60% can lose control if investors hold the board or key vetoes.

As a company grows, investors often want an experienced CEO from outside. Many founders accept this, but it can mean losing the top job in the company they started.

Two kinds of shares

Some founders keep control with dual-class shares: their shares carry more votes each than the shares sold to investors.

Google (Alphabet) and Facebook (Meta) both use this structure, so their founders kept voting control long after selling most of the company.

Investors do not always accept it, because it limits their say.

Private equity

Buying whole companies with borrowed money

Private equity (PE) funds usually buy whole, mature companies that already make a profit. They run them for a few years, try to make them more valuable, and then sell them.

A PE fund pays mostly with borrowed money, so the deal is called a leveraged buyout (LBO). The company itself carries the loans and repays them from its own cash.

Like a VC fund, a PE fund raises money from pension funds and other large investors, lasts about ten years, and charges a fee plus a share of the profits.

Borrowing makes the gains bigger when things go well, and the losses bigger when they do not. The chart below shows both cases.

A typical buyout has three steps: buy the company, improve it over three to seven years, then sell it to another firm, to another fund, or on the stock market.

When borrowing goes wrong

In 2007, the PE firms KKR and TPG, with Goldman Sachs, bought the Texas power company TXU for a record $32 billion, mostly with debt. Then the shale gas boom pushed gas and electricity prices down. The company could not carry its loans and went bankrupt in 2014 (BMAE 33-1).

Where the fund's gain comes from

In the good case the fund turns €40m into €120m. The €80m gain has two sources.

How PE tries to add value

  1. Pressure to perform. Large loan repayments push managers to cut waste and generate cash.
  2. Managers as owners. Managers own part of the company, so they gain when it does well. Kaplan (1989) found operating profits rose 24% on average in the three years after management buyouts.
  3. Close supervision. A few owners watch the company closely, unlike thousands of small shareholders.

VC and PE side by side

Both raise money in funds and both plan to sell within a few years. The difference is the kind of company they buy and how much they borrow.

FinTech

New technology, old financial services

FinTech means using new technology to offer financial services such as payments, loans and investing.

It has grown because of three things: more data about customers, artificial intelligence, and cheap cloud computing (BMAE 13-7).

Most FinTech start-ups are funded by venture capital, so everything in the VC section applies to them too.

PaymentsPaying by card or phone app instead of cash. In Sweden, cash is barely 2% of the value of all payments.
Online lendingWebsites that match borrowers with lenders, without a bank.
CrowdfundingMany people each invest a small amount in a start-up.
Credit scoringUsing data and AI to judge whether someone will repay a loan.
BlockchainA shared record kept on many computers that cannot be changed later.
CryptoDigital money such as Bitcoin. Prices swing widely and many projects fail.

An Irish example

Stripe handles online card payments for millions of businesses. It was founded in 2010 by brothers Patrick and John Collison from County Tipperary.

Its early backers included the VC firms Sequoia Capital and Andreessen Horowitz. Like many large FinTech firms, it has stayed private for years, funded by VC rounds instead of the stock market.

Advice for founders

Working with investors

Not all money is equal

Two investors can offer the same amount at the same valuation and still be very different partners. Founders compare:

  • Reputation. A well-known VC makes it easier to hire, win customers and raise the next round.
  • Help beyond money. Contacts, advice and experience, often called smart money.
  • Track record. Their past deals, and what founders they backed say about them.
  • Other offers. Getting term sheets from several investors shows what the company is really worth and strengthens the founders' hand.
  • Exclusivity. Some term sheets ban talks with other investors for a set period, and a few clauses bind as soon as it is signed. Read those parts first.

Every term is a trade

Good negotiators rarely just say no. They give something the other side values and ask for something in return.

If the founders acceptThey might ask for
A lower valuationSimpler terms, such as no participation
An investor seat on the boardA founder majority, or choosing the independent director together
Money paid in stagesGoals that are realistic and clearly measurable
Vesting on their sharesClear, fair rules for a founder who leaves for good reasons

A toolkit

Five questions to ask about any term sheet

1MoneyHow much comes in, at what valuation, and what share do the founders really keep after the option pool?
2ControlWho holds the votes, the board seats, the vetoes and the CEO job, now and after later rounds?
3The downsideIf the company is sold for a low price, who gets what? Work through a few sale prices.
4The futureHow do the money stages, goals and terms affect the next round, hiring and the exit?
5The relationshipWhat does this investor bring besides money, and what could the founders offer in return for better terms?

Test yourself

Self-check

A VC invests €3m in a company worth €9m before the deal. What share does it get?

The company is worth €12m after the deal, so the VC owns €3m ÷ €12m = 25%.

Why do VCs invest in stages?

So they can stop funding a company that is not reaching its goals.

How can a VC fund make money if most start-ups fail?

One or two big winners can return more than all the losses combined.

Can founders with less than half the shares still control the company?

Yes, if they hold most board seats, the CEO job, or shares with extra votes.

An option pool is created just before a VC invests. Who pays for it?

The existing owners. The new shares dilute them, while the investor's percentage is fixed by the deal.

Why is a leveraged buyout risky?

The debt must be repaid first. If the company's value falls, the fund's share can shrink to almost nothing.

Two investors both value a company at €10m. Why might the founders prefer one?

The other terms differ, such as who gets paid first, board control and vesting, and one investor may bring more help.

A founder's share falls from 80% to 60% after a new round. Is the founder worse off?

Not necessarily. If the company is now worth much more, a smaller share can be worth more money.
